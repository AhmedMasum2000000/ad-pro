Why Dhaka traffic makes billboards work harder than almost anywhere

The thing everyone complains about is the thing that makes the medium work. A stationary audience at a signalled junction is worth more per second than a motorway doing sixty — and Dhaka produces that condition all day.

3 February 2026 · 6 min read · Industry

There is a slightly uncomfortable truth at the centre of out-of-home in this city, and it is worth saying plainly: our medium is subsidised by everybody else’s misery.

Nobody enjoys forty minutes at Mohakhali. But those forty minutes are, in media terms, extraordinary. Attention is the scarcest commodity in advertising, and Dhaka manufactures it by the hour.

Volume is the wrong metric

Buyers new to the medium ask for the busiest road. It is intuitive and it is wrong, because volume and attention are different things and they often point in opposite directions.

The same audience, two different conditions
ConditionVehiclesTime in viewWhat lands
Fast arterial roadVery highUnder two secondsA logo, at best
Signalled junctionLowerThirty seconds to two minutesA logo, a line, and a second look
Flyover approachHighThree to six secondsA strong single idea
Market or bazaar roadModerate, slowSustained, repeatedDetail, and genuine recall

The same audience, two different conditions

A junction that queues gives you the one thing no other medium can guarantee: a person with nothing else to look at.

The angle matters as much as the address

Two screens can sit at the same junction and perform completely differently, because one faces the queue and the other faces the traffic already moving away. When we list a site as "facing Gulshan 2 and Mohakhali", that is not decoration — it tells you which flow of people has it in view, and for how long.

This is the single most common planning mistake I see. A brand books a famous junction, takes whichever face was free, and reaches the people leaving rather than the people arriving.

What this means for your creative

If you have genuinely bought dwell time, you can afford one more beat than the usual seven-word rule allows — a second line, a price, a location. Not a paragraph. But the standard advice, which is written for motorway media in countries where traffic moves, is slightly too austere for a Dhaka signal.

Every site page states which direction the face is pointing and what its on-air window is. Between those two facts you can work out who sees it and when, before anyone visits.

Tell us which side of the city your customers come from and we will find the face that catches them.
